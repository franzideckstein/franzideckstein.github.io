### Working under extreme heat  
<p style="margin-top:-12px; margin-bottom:0; font-size:0.9em;">

<details style="margin-top:10px;">
  <summary style="cursor:pointer; font-size:0.9em;">Abstract</summary>
  <p style="font-size:0.9em; text-align:justify;">
    As climate change intensifies, extreme heat events are becoming more frequent and intense. Heat has been shown to reduce labor productivity and economic output. However, most of this evidence is based on aggregate levels of estimation across countries. To uncover the mechanisms at play, individual-level analysis is required. Here, we show that individuals in Germany work 10 minutes more per week for every additional day exceeding 36$^{\circ}$ Celsius without achieving gains in earnings. Using rich and representative survey panel data that follows more than 50,000 individuals and their postal codes over decades, we statistically estimate the impact of different temperature ranges on people's time use. The response to local heat is driven by younger individuals, women and part-time workers. Time-use records suggest a shift from hobbies to work on weekends while weekday time allocation remains unchanged indicating that individuals compensate heat-induced productivity loss. Our micro-level results are backed by aggregate data on the county-level that confirm both the rise in working hours and the insignificant association with overall economic output. Our results imply welfare losses as individuals work more for the same income, thus demonstrating costs from warming in a country with temperate climate such as Germany.
  </p>
